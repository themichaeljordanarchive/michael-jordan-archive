The Nuances of the Jordan Jersey

A closer look at Chicago Bulls uniform inventory practices and the unexpected seasonal overlap revealed through photographic evidence.

Uniform Inventory and Carryover in the Late 1980s

When examining Michael Jordan’s game-worn jerseys, it is easy to assume that the uniforms worn during a particular season were produced specifically for that year. In reality, archival research often reveals a far more complicated story.

While studying imagery and artifacts connected to the 1989–90 Chicago Bulls season, a pattern slowly began to emerge. Several jerseys worn during that season appeared to originate from earlier Sand-Knit production runs, suggesting that the Bulls’ equipment inventory extended across multiple seasons.

One revealing moment comes from a team photo session following an April 1990 Bulls game. In the photographs from that session, three players can be seen wearing white home uniforms. At first glance they appear identical, but a closer examination of their tagging and construction tells a different story.

Scottie Pippen appears in a jersey consistent with 1986–87 Sand-Knit production. Standing nearby, Michael Jordan is wearing a home jersey tagged for 1988–89. In another image from the same session, Bill Cartwright is seen wearing a home uniform tagged for the 1987–88 production year. In a single moment captured late in the season, three different players appear to be wearing jerseys originating from multiple production cycles.

Additional evidence comes from two red road jerseys worn by Michael Jordan during the 1989–90 season. Both garments have surfaced publicly and have been conclusively photomatched to game imagery from that year. Together, the two jerseys account for more than thirty games of documented wear during the season.

What makes these artifacts particularly interesting is their tagging. One jersey carries 1986–87 Sand-Knit tagging, while the other originates from 1987–88 production. Despite being manufactured in earlier seasons, both uniforms were clearly in active use during the 1989–90 Bulls campaign.

Taken together, these five pieces of evidence suggest that the Chicago Bulls’ uniform inventory during this period likely included garments produced across several prior seasons. When a team’s uniform design remained unchanged, earlier jerseys could continue to circulate alongside newer stock.

For collectors and researchers, this nuance carries an important implication. The year printed on a Sand-Knit jersey tag does not necessarily correspond to the season in which the jersey was worn. Instead, the tag reflects the garment’s production cycle, which may precede its appearance on the court by several seasons.
